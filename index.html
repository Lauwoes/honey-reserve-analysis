The wholesale market pays for scale, not for flavour

A premium honey concept assumed the category ignores distinctiveness. Ten years of USDA records show honey prices vary by nearly a factor of four between states, but the thing they track is production volume, not the honey itself.

Supply is concentrated, and stable

Five states account for between 52 and 62 per cent of US honey production every year. Only seven states ever appear in that top five at all. North Dakota is in it all nine years.

The apparent flavour premium

California and Florida sit behind the varietals the brand concept names, almond and citrus. Both are among the lowest-yielding major producers, and both earn more per pound than the high-volume Northern Plains states. New York, at a fifth of North Dakota's yield in volume terms, earns 87 per cent more per pound.

It disappears when scale is controlled for

Yield looks like it drives price until production volume enters the model. Then the yield coefficient halves and stops being distinguishable from zero, while log production takes over.

Scale was doing the work.

A tenfold increase in a state's production is associated with roughly $1.64 per pound less in price received. Virginia does not earn $7.02 because its honey tastes better than North Dakota's; it earns $7.02 because it produces very little and sells close to retail, while North Dakota sells by the drum to packers.

What this changes about the brief

The premium the concept wants to capture does not exist upstream. There is no varietal price signal in the wholesale data at all, only a scale discount. The premium would have to be created downstream through packaging, story and retail position, on honey bought at commodity rates.

That is a viable strategy and it is what Graza and Carbone do, both named in the original deck. But it is a different argument from the one the deck makes. "We are surfacing value the category ignores" becomes "we are manufacturing value the category does not price", which carries different risks and needs different proof.

It also puts a number on the Bee Reserve proposal. Sourcing from small at-risk apiaries means buying in the $5 to $7 range rather than near $2.

Limitations

  • Price received is not retail price. The channel explanation, that small states sell direct while large ones sell to packers, is inference from the pattern rather than something USDA reports.
  • 41 states, one observation each. The regression is on state averages, so this is suggestive rather than decisive.
  • Varietal composition is not in the data. Reading California as almond and citrus honey comes from general knowledge, not from USDA. Evidencing it would need a separate source.
  • Hawaii is a genuine outlier at 104 lb per colony on a different production system, and the models should be reported with and without it.
  • Census and survey years are not mixed. USDA's five-yearly census uses different methods, so 2017 and 2022 census records are excluded. Years after 2023 are excluded because USDA has not finished publishing them, not because production fell.
